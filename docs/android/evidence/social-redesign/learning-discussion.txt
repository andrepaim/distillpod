“Rereading can make an idea feel familiar. Trying to recall it without looking shows what you can actually retrieve.”

Familiarity and recall are not the same measure of learning. The speaker draws a useful distinction between recognizing an idea while it is in front of us and being able to bring it to mind when it is not. That distinction changes what a productive study session might look like.

One implication is that ease can be misleading. A passage that feels clear on a second reading may still be difficult to explain with the page closed. In this interpretation, attempting recall is not simply a final test; it is a way to discover which parts of an idea remain accessible and which need another look.

A concrete approach would be to pause after reading, put the material aside, and write a brief explanation from memory. Then compare that explanation with the source and return to the exercise later, rather than treating one successful attempt as the finish line.

The speaker also includes an important qualification: the best approach depends on what needs to be learned. The practical question is not just whether the material feels familiar, but what you will need to retrieve or use.
